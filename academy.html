Tier 1 — Beginner

The Basics Every Business Owner Needs

Beginner

What Is Phishing and How to Spot It

Phishing is when an attacker sends a fake email pretending to be someone you trust — your bank, Microsoft, a vendor, or even your boss. The goal is to get you to click a link, enter a password, or wire money. These emails look real. They use real logos, real names, and create urgency so you act before you think. In 2024, over 90% of cyberattacks started with a phishing email.

Key Takeaway

Before clicking any link in an email, hover over it and look at the actual URL. If the domain looks wrong or unfamiliar, do not click it. When in doubt, go directly to the website by typing the address yourself.

Beginner

Social Engineering — Attacking People, Not Computers

Social engineering is manipulation. An attacker calls your front desk pretending to be IT support, a vendor, or even a government official. They create urgency, build false trust, and get your employee to hand over access, passwords, or money. No hacking required. Kevin Mitnick, one of the most famous hackers in history, said he almost never needed to break into a computer — people just let him in.

Key Takeaway

Create a simple rule for your business: no one gives out passwords, access, or makes wire transfers based on a phone call or email alone — always verify through a second channel.

Beginner

Why Your ISP Router Is Not a Firewall

When your internet provider installed your service, they left behind a router — a white or black plastic box that blinks. That router connects you to the internet. What it does not do is protect you from the internet. A real firewall inspects traffic, blocks threats, enforces rules, and logs what comes and goes. Your ISP router does none of that. It is a door with no lock.

Key Takeaway

If the only device between your business and the internet is the box your ISP gave you, you have no real network security. A business-grade firewall is the single most important security investment you can make.

Beginner

What a Firewall Actually Does

A firewall is a gatekeeper between your network and the internet. It inspects every packet of data going in and out, compares it against a set of rules, and decides whether to allow or block it. Modern firewalls also do intrusion detection, application filtering, and VPN termination. The difference between a $60 consumer router and a $600 business firewall is not speed — it is visibility, control, and protection.

Key Takeaway

A firewall is not optional for a business. It is the foundation everything else is built on. Without it, everything on your network is exposed.

Beginner

VLANs — Why Your Network Should Be Divided

A VLAN is a Virtual Local Area Network — a way to divide one physical network into separate isolated segments. Think of it like floors in a building with locked stairwells between them. Your point-of-sale system should be on its own VLAN. Your cameras should be on their own VLAN. Your guest Wi-Fi should be completely isolated. Without VLANs, everything on your network can see everything else — and so can an attacker who gets in.

Key Takeaway

If your cameras, registers, employee computers, and guest Wi-Fi are all on the same network, a compromise of any one of them is a compromise of all of them.

Beginner

Guest Wi-Fi Done Wrong

Almost every small business offers guest Wi-Fi. Almost none of them set it up correctly. Guest Wi-Fi that runs on the same network as your business systems is not guest Wi-Fi — it is an open door to everything you own. A customer on your guest network should only be able to reach the internet, nothing else. No printers, no registers, no servers, no cameras. This requires proper VLAN configuration and a firewall — not just a separate password on the same router.

Key Takeaway

Ask your IT person or internet provider one question: is the guest Wi-Fi on a completely separate VLAN from our business systems? If they hesitate or say yes without being able to explain it, you have a problem.

Tier 2 — Intermediate

Go Deeper — Common Attack Scenarios

Intermediate

Ransomware — What Actually Happens Step by Step

Ransomware is malware that encrypts your files and demands payment to restore them. But it does not happen instantly. An attacker typically gets in through a phishing email or unpatched vulnerability, then spends days or weeks quietly moving through your network before triggering the encryption. By the time you see the ransom note, your backups may already be compromised. The average ransom demand for small businesses in 2024 was over $200,000.

Key Takeaway

The best ransomware defense is network segmentation, regular offline backups, and patched systems. If your backups are on the same network as your computers, they will be encrypted too.

Intermediate

Why Security Cameras Are a Security Risk

Security cameras are supposed to protect your business. But most small business camera installations connect the cameras directly to the main business network with no isolation. IP cameras are notoriously insecure — many run outdated firmware with known vulnerabilities and default passwords that were never changed. An attacker who compromises a camera on your main network now has a foothold inside your business. In 2021, a hacker accessed 150,000 cameras across hospitals, jails, and businesses through a single vendor's weak credentials.

Key Takeaway

Cameras should always be on their own isolated VLAN with no access to the rest of your network. They should reach the internet only if remote viewing is required, and only through a controlled path.

Intermediate

Vendor Access — The Hidden Risk

Your HVAC company needs to check the thermostat. Your POS vendor needs remote access to run an update. Your cleaning crew knows your Wi-Fi password. Every person you give network access to is a potential entry point — not because they are malicious, but because their devices, accounts, and credentials may be compromised without their knowledge. The Target breach in 2013, which exposed 40 million credit cards, started through a heating and air conditioning vendor.

Key Takeaway

Vendors should never be on your main business network. Create a separate isolated VLAN for vendor access or use a temporary guest credential that expires after their visit.

Intermediate

How to Read a Suspicious Email

The from name in an email can say anything — attackers set it to whatever they want. What matters is the actual sending domain. Look at the address in angle brackets, not just the display name. A fake email might show "Microsoft Support" as the name but send from support@micros0ft-help.ru. Other red flags include urgency and threats, requests for passwords or wire transfers, links that do not match the supposed sender, and attachments you were not expecting.

Key Takeaway

Check the actual sending domain, not the display name. Hover over links before clicking. When in doubt, call the sender directly using a number you already know — not one from the email.

Tier 3 — Advanced

Business Owner Essentials

Advanced

PCI DSS Basics — What Card-Processing Businesses Must Know

If your business accepts credit or debit cards, you are required to comply with PCI DSS — the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard. This is not optional. It requires that your card-processing systems be isolated from the rest of your network, that you use strong encryption, that you maintain logs, and that you regularly test your security. Non-compliance can result in fines, lost ability to accept cards, and liability for fraudulent charges after a breach.

Key Takeaway

Your point-of-sale system must be on its own isolated network segment. If it shares a network with your guest Wi-Fi, cameras, or employee computers, you are likely out of PCI compliance right now.

Advanced

Incident Response — The First 60 Minutes

When a breach happens, most businesses waste the most critical time because they have no plan. The first 60 minutes determine whether an incident becomes a disaster. Step one is isolation — disconnect affected systems from the network immediately. Step two is documentation — take photos of error messages, do not delete anything. Step three is notification — contact your IT provider, your bank if financial accounts may be involved, and your cyber insurance provider if you have one. Do not pay a ransom without legal and insurance consultation.

Key Takeaway

Write a one-page incident response plan before you need it. It should include who to call, how to isolate systems, and what not to do. A plan made in a crisis is not a plan.

Advanced

How to Know If Your Network Is Actually Secure

Most small business owners assume their network is secure because nothing bad has happened yet. That is not security — that is luck. A truly secure network has a business-grade firewall with active rules, VLANs separating critical systems, documented credentials stored securely, regular firmware updates on all devices, and someone who can answer detailed questions about the configuration. If your IT person cannot show you a network diagram and a firewall rule summary, you do not know what you have.

Key Takeaway

Security is not a product you buy once. It is a configuration you maintain. Schedule an annual review of your network the same way you schedule an annual review of your finances.
